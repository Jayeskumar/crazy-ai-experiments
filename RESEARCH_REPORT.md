# Can a neural network learn from another network's weights alone?

*An A → B → C experiment on tiny character-level language models. Everything reported
here was executed in this repository, on a 4-core CPU cloud container with no GPU. Every number
comes from `results/summary.json` / `results/tables.md`, which are regenerated by `src/analysis.py`.*

## 1. Summary

**Short answer.** A weight-only learner (Model B) *can* emit weights for a new
network (Model C) that does language well, with no text training, **but only when the
source networks share training history.** In our setting, everything B produced that worked can be
explained as returning the centre of a population of mutually connected solutions, or
moving inside it. We found no evidence that B transmits model-specific knowledge. We also found no
evidence that it discovers language structure independently of what is already present, in
averaged form, in its training weights.

| source population for B | what Model C does | why |
|---|---|---|
| 96 **independently initialised** Model A instances | **fails completely.** B reconstructions of held-out models: test loss 7.87 nats/char (uniform guessing = 3.22), 0% grammatical sentences. B's reconstructions of its *own training models* also fail (loss 2.05). | Independent solutions are separated by a loss barrier that data-free permutation alignment does not remove (midpoint loss 3.64). Across models, the deviations from the mean share almost no low-dimensional structure (top-16 PCs explain 0.2% of held-out variance). |
| 24 models sharing only the **initialisation** | **Partial.** Loss 0.461 vs 0.417 for the originals. Structure is mostly kept (84% of sentences structurally valid) but subject–verb agreement drops to chance (0.51). | Small barrier (midpoint 0.440). B returns the population centre, which sits on that barrier. |
| 24 models **spawned** from a shared checkpoint at step 300 or 1000 | **Full success.** B reconstructions and new B samples: loss 0.416, 96–98% grammatical, agreement 1.00. Matches or slightly beats the originals (0.417). | Linearly connected solutions (no barrier). The population mean is itself a good model ("model soup"), and B reproduces it. |
| one training **trajectory** (29 late snapshots of one run) | Produces a working LM for *any* input, but that LM is the memorised run itself. | Outputs for 16 different held-out inputs differ by only 1.4% of the weight norm, while the inputs differ from the outputs by 111%. B has become a constant function. |

Main negative findings:
1. B never beat the **trivial baseline "population mean"** at reconstructing held-out models, in any population. Its reconstruction error is within 1–3% of the mean's. Given a held-out model, B returns (almost) the same output whatever the input.
2. B never beat **linear PCA** (the linear autoencoder) in any way that mattered: reconstruction, masked-tensor recovery, latent-size sweeps, or directed mutation.
3. "Extreme compression" is trivially achievable in the families: **0 bytes per model** (the population mean) works as well as any latent size. No model-specific information is being compressed. The capability lives in B's decoder, which is 15.1M parameters, 518× larger than one Model A.

Main positive findings:
1. A strict, tested data-isolation boundary is feasible, and *under it*, weight-only generation of a working language model does happen (in the spawned populations). Model C produced grammatical text such as `"the small farmers near the small farmer wait. the foxes behind the child run. every dog waits."` from weights generated by a network that never saw a character.
2. B's latent space encodes a useful **weight-space geometry**. Mutations along B's latent directions damaged models about 2–8× less than norm-matched random mutations, and in the shared-init population they *improved* B's starting reconstruction in 71–73% of cases. PCA directions behave about the same, so this is a property of the population's spread rather than something B uniquely discovered.
3. B-sampled models are **new**, not copies: they are functionally distinct from one another and from every training model. But they sit *between* the training models (closer to the train-ensemble than any real model is), i.e. they behave like soups/interpolations.

## 2. Setup

### 2.1 Language and Model A
- **Synthetic language** (`src/data.py`): a probabilistic grammar with determiners, adjectives, 6 nouns,
  4 intransitive + 4 transitive verbs, optional prepositional phrases whose noun has independent number
  ("attractor"), and subject–verb + determiner–noun **number agreement**. 25 characters. Train / val /
  test corpora (300k / 30k / 30k chars) come from different seeds.
  The **exact entropy rate** of the source (Monte-Carlo over exact sentence probabilities) is **0.4135 nats/char**,
  which is the irreducible loss.
- **Model A** (`src/model_a.py`): 2-layer, single-head, d=32, MLP 128, context 64, LayerNorm. **29,145 parameters**
  (116.6 KB in fp32). AdamW, 3,000 steps, batch 32, cosine LR. ~34 s per model on one CPU thread.
- **Evaluation** (`src/evaluate.py`): test loss and perplexity (each scored char has ≥32 chars of context);
  1,500 sampled characters (T=0.8, fixed seed) scored by an exact grammar checker (fraction of sentences that are
  structurally valid / fully grammatical); 200 agreement **minimal pairs** (plain, and with an attractor noun of
  opposite number); generation latency.
- **Model A learns the language.** Held-out independent models: test loss **0.418 ± 0.001** (0.004 nats above
  the entropy floor; bigram model 1.641, unigram 2.882), 95% grammatical generated sentences, agreement accuracy
  0.999 (plain) / 0.981 (attractor). Samples, e.g. *"the small farmer sings. the old farmer with the cat sings. one
  cat chases one bird. a child waits."*

### 2.2 Populations of Model A (169 trained networks + 32 snapshots)
| population | n | what is shared | val loss |
|---|---|---|---|
| `ind` | 96 | nothing (different init seed and data order). Split for B: 64 train / 16 val / 16 test | 0.4196 ± 0.0015 |
| `shared` | 24 | identical initialisation, different data order | 0.4195 ± 0.0009 |
| `spawn300` | 24 | identical first 300 steps (10%), then different data order | 0.4189 ± 0.0004 |
| `spawn1000` | 24 | identical first 1,000 steps (33%) | 0.4189 ± 0.0004 |
| `traj` | 32 | one run, snapshots every 25 steps over the last 800 steps | — |

For families, B trains on 14 models, validates on 2 and is tested on 8 held-out models.
The `shared`/`spawn` populations were **added during the study** after the first diagnostics showed that
independent models are not linearly connected (Section 4.1). This was a targeted, evidence-driven change.

### 2.3 Model B (weights only)
- **Input/target:** a flattened 29,145-dim parameter vector. Normalisation: per-coordinate population mean,
  per-tensor population std. Both are statistics of weights only and count as part of B.
- **Architecture** (`src/model_b.py`): MLP autoencoder, 29,145 → 256 → 256 → k → 256 → 256 → 29,145, GELU,
  dropout. **15.09M parameters.**
- **Objective** (`src/train_b.py`): MSE reconstruction with three regularisers. (i) Each whole parameter
  tensor is masked to the mean with probability 0.2 (masked-tensor recovery). (ii) Gaussian input noise
  (denoising). (iii) Latent noise plus an L2 penalty, so the latent space can be sampled from. 2,000 steps, AdamW.
  The checkpoint is chosen by **weight-space** validation MSE only. 28 B variants were trained, ~400 s each.
- **Sampling:** a Gaussian (shrinkage covariance) is fit to the training latents, then sampled and decoded.
- **Alignment** (`src/alignment.py`): data-free permutation weight matching (Git Re-Basin coordinate ascent)
  over all exact permutation symmetries: residual stream, q/k dims, v/o dims, MLP units. The template is the iterated
  mean of aligned training models. A second, *continuous* gauge fix was tested as well: an SVD canonical form of the
  invariant products W_qᵀW_k and W_oW_v. Both are verified to be exact function-preserving symmetries
  (`tests/test_alignment.py`; the canonicalisation leaves the loss unchanged to 1e-9).
- **Controls with identical interface:** PCA (linear autoencoder) with the same k; population mean;
  per-coordinate diagonal Gaussian; pairwise interpolation; int8 / int4 (per-row) quantisation;
  per-matrix truncated SVD (rank 4 and 8).

### 2.4 Data isolation (what B could and could not see)
B ran in a process that first called `enter_weights_only_mode()`. That call installs (a) an import blocker for
every text-side module and (b) an irremovable `sys.addaudithook` that raises on opening anything under `runs/`
(checkpoints, vocabulary, samples, metrics) or `results/language/`. The worker processes are forked and inherit both.
B's only input is `artifacts/weights_only/*.npz`, written by a separate sanitiser. The sanitiser loads only
`weights.pt` state dicts (`torch.load(weights_only=True)`), rejects any non-float tensor or unexpected key, and
writes exactly `{W, names, shapes, run_ids, split}`. B re-validates that schema on load.
`tests/test_isolation.py` (15 tests) verifies blocked imports and blocked file access in subprocesses, sanitiser
rejection of non-weight content, the schema of the real sanitised files, and (by static AST check) that no B-side
module imports a text-side module. B saw: weight values, parameter names, shapes and order, and opaque run ids.
B did **not** see: text, tokens, the vocabulary, logits, activations, samples or losses.

**Remaining leak channels, stated honestly.** (1) The *researcher* (me) saw language-side results and then
chose to add the shared/spawned populations and the gauge-fixing variant. That is a human-level, not a gradient-level,
use of language feedback. (2) The embedding/head shapes reveal the vocabulary *size* (25), which the brief allows.
(3) Model C uses A's tokenizer at inference, as the brief allows.

## 4. Why: the weight-alignment problem (investigated explicitly)

### 4.1 Independent solutions are not connected, even after alignment
Test loss along w(α) = (1−α)w₁ + αw₂ for 4 pairs (endpoints ≈ 0.418):

| pair type | α=0.25 | α=0.5 | α=0.75 |
|---|---|---|---|
| independent, raw | 2.217 | 3.587 | 2.141 |
| independent, permutation-aligned | 2.066 | 3.637 | 1.833 |
| independent, gauge-fixed + aligned | 2.217 | 3.707 | 2.176 |
| shared initialisation | 0.427 | **0.440** | 0.427 |
| spawned at step 300 | 0.416 | **0.416** | 0.416 |
| spawned at step 1000 | 0.416 | **0.416** | 0.416 |

- Weight matching reduces the mean pairwise distance only from 1.180 to 1.112 (relative to the weight norm). It also
  leaves the barrier at the midpoint (≈3.6, above uniform). When matching two independent models it finds the identity
  permutation for only 3.8% of units, which is chance level. For shared/spawned pairs it returns the **identity
  permutation for 100%** of units. So the matching algorithm works where solutions are related; it just cannot make
  unrelated solutions related.
- The SVD gauge fix for the continuous q/k and v/o symmetries is exact but does **not** reduce the barrier. The barrier
  is therefore not an artefact of those symmetries. Remaining explanations, none of which we could separate here:
  the network is too narrow (Git Re-Basin reports that barriers fall with width; d=32 is very narrow), weight matching
  is a poor proxy for functional matching in Transformers, or the solutions differ by more than symmetries.

### 4.2 There is little shared low-dimensional structure even within a basin
Fraction of a held-out model's deviation from the training mean explained by the training population's top-16 PCs:
**0.2%** (independent, aligned or not), 4.2% (shared), 4.6% (spawn300), 0.8% (spawn1000). Trained models scatter
around their population centre in directions that are almost unique to each model. With 14–64 examples in
29,145 dimensions, any autoencoder that has to generalise can at best output the centre plus a small shared
correction. B's validation MSE confirms this: 1.04 for independent models, in units where "predict the mean" ≈ 1.0.

### 4.3 Why one training checkpoint (or one trajectory) is insufficient
A single checkpoint is a single point. Any learner fitted to it can only learn that point (or noise around it), and
"reconstruction" of other models degenerates into emitting it. We tested the stronger version, a whole late
trajectory of one run (as in checkpoint-diffusion work). B_traj reproduces its own held-out snapshots almost perfectly
(rel. error 0.8%, loss 0.417). For 16 held-out *independent* models it emits working LMs (loss 0.420, 89%
grammatical), but its outputs for different inputs differ by only **1.4%** of the weight norm, while they are **111%**
away from the input they were supposed to reconstruct. In function space, the outputs are closer to the trajectory
model (KL 0.0099) than to the input model (KL 0.0146). That looks like generalisation but is memorisation. Telling
weight-space *learning* from storage needs many independent examples of the structure, held-out examples, and the
"emit the training point" control.

Observations:
- **Capability ordering under damage (shared family):** word validity and sentence structure survive (84% structurally
  valid), but number agreement collapses to chance (plain 0.51, attractor 0.64). The longest-range dependency in the
  language is the first capability lost when moving from a real solution to the population centre.
- B samples are *better* than the shared-family mean (0.454 vs 0.464) and much better than PCA samples (0.507).
  B samples are spread around the centre but drawn towards real models (nearest-train-A KL 0.12 vs 0.17 for the mean).
- In spawned families, weight averaging ≈ functional ensembling: KL(population-mean model ‖ ensemble of 14 train
  models) = 0.0003, versus 0.0018 for a real held-out model. The mean (0.4162) is very slightly better than individual
  models (0.4170). B reproduces this (it is a soup effect, not B learning anything new).
- **Cross-population transfer:** feeding an independent model into a family-trained B returns the family centre
  (relative error 1.15–1.18) with family-level quality. More direct evidence that B's output does not depend on its input.

## 6. The five research questions

**Exp 1 — How much language ability survives transmission through B?** For *held-out* models: none for independent
sources (worse than random init); about 96% of the bigram→A loss gap for shared-init sources, but with agreement at
chance; 100% for spawned sources. In no case did the *individual* model's information survive (B ≈ population mean;
relative error within 1–3% of the mean's). What survives is the capability held in common by the population, and
only when the population's mean is itself a solution.

**Exp 2 — Extreme compression.** Latent size k ∈ {1,…,64} (independent) and {2,4,8,16} (families) made no consistent
difference in the families: every k gives the population-mean behaviour (spawn300: 0.416 for all k). In the independent
population every k fails. Odd but irrelevant detail: k=1–4 fail *less* badly (4.8–6.4) than k≥16 (≈8), because
smaller-k decoders are less sharp. The honest answer: **0 bytes per model** suffice wherever anything works, because B
transmits no model-specific content. The "compression" is entirely in a 60 MB decoder for 117 KB models.

**Exp 3 — Directed vs random mutation.** Variants were generated by perturbing B's latent (scale 0.5 / 1.0 of the
training-latent covariance) and compared with random perturbations of equal per-tensor norm and PCA-direction
perturbations of equal total norm (8 models × 6 variants × 2 scales per population):

| population | Δ test loss: B-latent | random on B-recon | PCA dirs | B's Δ added to original A | random added to A |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| shared, scale 1.0 | **−0.0072** (71% improve) | +0.169 (0% improve) | −0.0005 | +0.051 | +0.169 |
| spawn300, scale 1.0 | +0.0006 | +0.0047 | +0.0004 | +0.0013 | +0.0062 |
| spawn1000, scale 1.0 | +0.0005 | +0.0010 | +0.0006 | +0.0008 | +0.0014 |

Directions that B learned are functionally "flat": about 2–8× less harmful than random directions of the same size (3.3× in the shared family when added to the original A), and
B-directed moves away from the (barrier-sitting) shared-family centre improve it. **No variant beat the original
Model A.** The best-by-validation B variant in the shared family is still 0.019 above A on test loss. The
improvements are recovery towards real solutions, not new capability. PCA directions are about as good as B's (better
in spawned families), so this reflects the population's spread, not a nonlinear discovery. In the independent
population every "improvement" is just moving away from a confidently wrong centre (Δ ≈ −4, final losses ≈ 3.5–4,
still worse than uniform).

**Exp 4 — Multiple source models.** (a) More independent source models made B *worse*. On held-out independent
models, loss is 3.72 (n=4), 3.21 (n=8), 4.50 (16), 6.75 (32), 8.04 (64), the same trend as the aligned mean itself.
(b) Shared training history is what makes populations learnable. Measured by midpoint barrier, distance to the
centre (0.79 → 0.30 → 0.12 → 0.05 for independent → shared → spawn300 → spawn1000) and the quality of B's output.
B learned the structure *common to related models*. It did not learn structure common to unrelated models (beyond
what is shared after alignment, which is not functional).

**Exp 5 — Reproducible behaviour that differs from copying, interpolation and random init?** Measured in function
space (KL on test text; reference: two real models in a population differ by 0.003–0.011 nats/char):
- Copying is ruled out: B samples are nearest to 7 of 8 *different* training models (spawn300), and their nearest-model
  KL (0.0013) is well above 0, as it would be for a copy. It is about half a real held-out model's nearest-model KL
  (0.0027), i.e. the samples are closer to the training set as a whole, which fits lying *between* training models.
- Random init is ruled out wherever B works.
- Interpolation is **not** ruled out. B samples sit where pairwise midpoints sit: KL to the train-ensemble 0.0008 vs
  0.0008 for midpoints (spawn300); diversity within the sample group 0.0013 vs 0.0013. In the shared family B samples
  (0.454) are slightly worse than pairwise midpoints (0.447) and better than the 14-model mean (0.464).
- The behaviours reproduce across B seeds (independent C3: 8.04 / 7.87 / 7.71; spawned families: deterministic).
  We see nothing beyond population-centre / interpolation behaviour, and **we make no claim of new or emergent
  intelligence.**

## 7. Masked-tensor recovery (can B regenerate a missing part of a held-out model?)
Whole tensors (head, token embedding, positional embedding, a layer's attention, a layer's MLP) were hidden from B,
and B's prediction was spliced back into the original model. **B = PCA = filling with the population mean**, to
within noise, in every population and every group (e.g. shared, block-1 MLP: B 0.436, PCA 0.435, mean 0.437; random
fill 1.457). In spawned/shared families any of these give a working model. In the independent population none do
(head: 2.27; token embedding: 6.8). B did not exploit cross-tensor correlations beyond the mean: those correlations are
not shared across models.

## 8. Failure modes identified
1. **Basin mismatch** (independent sources). The population mean is not a solution, and B regresses to the mean.
2. **Regression to the mean / input-ignoring decoder.** Even where the mean works, B's output barely depends on its
   input (within-group KL among reconstructions 0.0001–0.003 vs 0.003–0.006 among real models).
3. **Memorisation that looks like generalisation** (single trajectory): working outputs, but constant regardless of input.
4. **Confidently-wrong averaging.** Averages of aligned independent models (and B's outputs) are far worse than
   uniform, and get worse as more models are averaged.
5. **Selective capability loss:** on the barrier (shared family), long-range agreement is lost first.

## 9. Limitations
- One tiny architecture (29k parameters, d=32). Wider models are known to have smaller post-alignment barriers; the
  negative result for independent sources may not hold at larger width. Untested here because of CPU budget.
- One synthetic language. No test on natural text.
- Model B is a plain MLP autoencoder on flattened weights. Permutation-equivariant weight-space networks (DWSNets / NFN),
  transformer-over-neurons encoders (SANE-style) and diffusion priors were not tried. They handle symmetry differently,
  but they cannot remove a loss barrier between basins. Alignment alone (4.1) and PCA (4.2) suggest the bottleneck is
  the data, not B's capacity.
- Only 14 training models per family and 64 independent ones; 8–16 held-out models per evaluation; 3 B seeds only for
  the main independent condition (families use 1 B seed). The Model A populations use one seed set.
- Activation-based alignment (which works better for Transformers) was deliberately excluded because it needs text.
- The populations were extended after the first results were seen (Section 2.4). That decision was made by me, using
  language results.
- Model A and Model B binaries are not committed (49 MB and 799 MB). Three representative Model A checkpoints are in
  `checkpoints/`; everything else regenerates from the fixed seeds in `configs/` and the code (README). CPU
  multithreading can make re-runs differ in the last digits.

## 10. Relation to existing literature (novelty assessment)

None of the ingredients here is new; the contribution is a small, controlled, fully
isolated A→B→C measurement on language models. Cited from memory and stated at the level
of general claims only, with no specific numbers taken from them:

- **Hypernetworks** (Ha, Dai & Le, 2016) generate weights of a target network, but are
  trained end-to-end through the *target's task loss*. That is exactly what B is forbidden
  to do here. Graph HyperNetworks / parameter prediction (Zhang et al. 2019; Knyazev et al. 2021)
  also train through task loss.
- **Model zoos and hyper-representations** (Schürholt et al. 2021–2024, incl. SANE) train
  autoencoders on populations of trained weights and sample new weights from the latent
  space. They are the closest prior work to Model B. Several of these works initialize the
  zoo models from shared or related seeds or use them as initializations for fine-tuning.
  Our finding that within-family sampling works while cross-basin sampling does not fits
  the role that population structure plays there.
- **Generative models of checkpoints**: G.pt (Peebles et al. 2022) learns a conditional
  diffusion model over checkpoints *with loss/metric conditioning*. p-diff (Wang et al. 2024,
  "Neural Network Diffusion") trains an autoencoder + latent diffusion on checkpoints
  collected from the *last epochs of a single training run* and reports generated models
  that perform like the originals. Our "single trajectory" and "spawn@1000" controls test
  that regime directly. HyperDiffusion (Erkoç et al. 2023) does the same for implicit fields.
- **Weight-space symmetry / alignment**: permutation symmetry and the conjecture that
  modulo permutations SGD solutions are linearly connected (Entezari et al. 2021); Git
  Re-Basin weight matching (Ainsworth et al. 2022), which reports that the barrier after
  alignment falls with width and is large for narrow models; linear mode connectivity and
  the spawning/stability analysis (Frankle et al. 2020); model soups (Wortsman et al. 2022),
  which average fine-tuned models that share a pre-trained initialization. Transformer
  merging across different initializations is known to need more than weight matching
  (e.g. activation-based matching), which B may not use here because activations require text.
- **Equivariant weight-space networks** (DWSNets, Navon et al. 2023; Neural Functionals,
  Zhou et al. 2023) build permutation symmetry into the architecture instead of aligning
  first. We did not implement these; see Limitations.

What this study adds is narrow: (i) a strict, *tested* data-isolation boundary between the
weight learner and anything linguistic; (ii) a direct measurement, on character-level
language models, of how the success of weight-only generation depends on how much training
history the source models share (independent → shared init → spawned at 10% → spawned at
33% → single trajectory); (iii) controls that separate "generation" from "copying the
population centre" in both weight space and function space.
